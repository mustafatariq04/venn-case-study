01 · Understanding Venn

Venn has built the banking layer. The spend layer is missing its engine.

Key finding

Everything Venn does well is about moving and holding a customer's money more cheaply than a bank. What it doesn't yet do is fund spend beyond what a customer has deposited. Rob described Venn as Mercury plus Ramp; Venn has Mercury's core and Ramp's surface, but not Ramp's credit engine.

How Venn makes money

Venn earns when money sits in it, moves through it, or is spent on its cards. Monthly fees are the smallest lever; the free plan exists to bring balances and transactions onto the platform.

Revenue streamPublic pricing signalGrows with
FX spread0.45% / 0.35% / 0.25% by planCross-border volume
Card interchangeVenn returns 1% cashback, capped at $5K and $25K monthly spend on the two lower plansCard spend
Net interest on depositsPays 2% on CAD and USD; a promotion offers up to 3.5% on net-new balancesBalances held
Subscriptions$0 / $40 / $100 per month, custom aboveUsers, controls, team size
Payment feesWires $6–10; EFT $2 on the free plan; invoice card payments 2.9% + $0.20Payment volume

What this tells me: ARPU depends on balances, card spend and FX volume, not seats. The plan structure is a deliberate ladder: lower FX rates, higher cashback caps and more controls pull customers up as they grow. A new product should be judged on whether it puts more of a customer's money flows through Venn.

The Mercury + Ramp lens

The Mercury model earns on deposits and money movement. The Ramp model earns on card spend that it funds with credit. Placing Venn's products against both shows where the gaps are.

What Venn does well

Venn wins by being much cheaper and faster than the Big 5 banks on the things Canadian SMBs feel every month: FX, USD banking, onboarding and team spend. These strengths are the assets any new product should build on.

StrengthEvidenceWhy it matters for what we build next

Price leadership on FX

Evidence0.25–0.45% markup vs 2–3% at legacy banks. One customer reports FX 3–4% cheaper than their old bank.

So whatVenn already attracts cross-border-heavy businesses. The next product should deepen that wallet, not only widen the funnel.

Real cross-border rails

EvidenceA US-domiciled USD account with local ACH. Big 5 USD accounts are Canada-based and SWIFT-only.

So whatA structural advantage banks can't copy quickly, and a reason to make Venn the primary operating account.

Frictionless onboarding

EvidenceSign-up in under 5 minutes with no personal credit check or guarantee. Banks take 1–2 weeks.

So whatFast acquisition is an asset to protect. Anything involving credit adds underwriting friction, so it needs careful design.

One platform instead of five tools

EvidenceAccounts, cards, expense management, payables, invoicing and QuickBooks/Xero sync in one place.

So whatEach added product raises switching costs and ARPU. The "default platform" strategy is already in motion.

Spend controls teams adopt

EvidenceInstant virtual cards, per-card and per-transaction limits, departments, approval rules, AI receipt matching.

So whatThe spend tooling is mature. What limits card spend today is the prepaid model, not the software.

Shipping velocity

Evidence35+ updates in 2026, including an AI assistant, iOS and Android apps, SSO, multi-entity and bulk payments.

So whatThe team can ship. The constraint on the next bet is regulatory and capital risk, not engineering speed.

Aggressive deposit growth

Evidence2% base interest, a promotion up to 3.5%, and no minimum balance.

So whatBalances are treated as a strategic asset. They are also the cash-flow data a future credit product would underwrite on.

Moving upmarket

EvidenceMid-market and enterprise offerings, SSO, custom roles and custom ERP integrations.

So whatLarger customers spend more than they keep on deposit, which makes the prepaid ceiling more visible.

The pattern: Venn's strengths are all about efficiency on money the customer already has. Growth from here comes from attracting more customers with that promise, or deepening each relationship by solving problems beyond cheaper money movement.

Sources: Venn home, pricing, Fall 2026 updates, Venn vs Big 5

What Venn optimizes for

The two company goals pull in different directions, and the best bet serves both. More active businesses widens the base; higher ARPU deepens each relationship.

  • Active businesses: at least one revenue-generating transaction in a month. A sign-up that never funds or spends doesn't count.
  • ARPU: grown by deepening product adoption, meaning more balances, spend and payment flows per customer.
  • ICP: Canadian-incorporated, 2–100 employees, outside Quebec. I assume the Quebec exclusion reflects its distinct language and regulatory requirements; that is a hypothesis.
  • Long-term strategy: the default banking and finance platform for Canadian businesses, 10x better and 10x cheaper than the status quo.

Hypotheses to validate

These are the questions I would answer with Venn's internal data before committing to a bet.

  1. The prepaid ceiling is costing card spend.TestCard spend relative to balance, and declines caused by insufficient funds.
  2. Many customers are FX-first rather than primary-account users.TestShare of active businesses whose payroll, rent or tax payments leave from Venn.
  3. "Active" and "signed up" differ meaningfully.TestMonthly active rate by sign-up cohort and plan.
  4. The card program is already credit-adjacent. Venn's public disclosure calls it a charge card, while the case describes a pre-funded model.TestConfirm the current program and what credit would require from the issuing partner.
  5. Reimbursements are partially built. Today they appear only on the Custom plan.TestDemand from lower plans, and how much of the workflow already exists.
