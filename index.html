01 · Understanding Venn

Venn has built the banking layer. The spend layer is missing its engine.

1.1Snapshot1.2Business model1.3Mercury + Ramp lens1.4Strengths1.5Goals1.6Hypotheses1.7Criteria

Key finding

Everything Venn does well is about moving and holding a customer's money more cheaply than a bank. What it doesn't yet do is fund spend beyond what a customer has deposited. Rob described Venn as Mercury plus Ramp; Venn has Mercury's core and Ramp's surface, but not Ramp's credit engine.

1.5What Venn optimizes for

The two company goals pull in different directions, and the best bet serves both. More active businesses widens the base; higher ARPU deepens each relationship.

  • Active businesses: at least one revenue-generating transaction in a month. A sign-up that never funds or spends doesn't count.
  • ARPU: grown by deepening product adoption, meaning more balances, spend and payment flows per customer.
  • ICP: Canadian-incorporated, 2–100 employees, outside Quebec. I assume the Quebec exclusion reflects its distinct language and regulatory requirements; that is a hypothesis.
  • Long-term strategy: the default banking and finance platform for Canadian businesses, 10x better and 10x cheaper than the status quo.

1.6Hypotheses to validate

These are the questions I would answer with Venn's internal data before committing to a bet.

  1. H1
    The prepaid ceiling is costing card spend.

    Customers keep only part of their operating cash in Venn, so card spend is capped below real demand.

    How I'd test it

    Card spend relative to balance, and declines caused by insufficient funds.

    Informs

    Credit cards
  2. H2
    Many customers are FX-first, not primary-account users.

    They come for cheap FX and keep their main account at a bank.

    How I'd test it

    Share of active businesses whose payroll, rent or tax payments leave from Venn.

    Informs

    Payroll
  3. H3
    "Active" and "signed up" differ meaningfully.

    The 15,000+ figure likely counts sign-ups, not monthly actives.

    How I'd test it

    Monthly active rate by sign-up cohort and plan.

    Informs

    All three
  4. H4
    The card program is already credit-adjacent.

    Venn's public disclosure calls it a charge card, while the case describes a pre-funded model.

    How I'd test it

    Confirm the current program and what credit would require from the issuing partner.

    Informs

    Credit cards
  5. H5
    Reimbursements are partially built.

    Today they appear only on the Custom plan.

    How I'd test it

2.5What SMBs say about credit

Canada's national SME financing survey adds a useful nuance. Demand for credit is real but not universal, and the cost of revolving card debt is high.

  • 49% of SMEs requested external financing in 2023, close to the pre-pandemic 47%.
  • Of those that did not request it, 81% said they didn't need it.
  • Business credit cards cost an average of 19% interest, against 11% for lines of credit.
  • Approval rates were high (90.9% for debt financing) and rise with firm size.

Key insight · Credit demand

Customers want the convenience of a credit card more than the borrowing.

SoA charge card paid in full each month (spend now, settle from the Venn account later, no revolving interest) fits that need and fits Venn's balance-driven model.

Source: ISED, Survey on Financing and Growth of SMEs 2023

2.6Reading the customer survey critically

The survey tells us what to investigate, not what to build. Before weighting the three requests, I would want to know:

  • Who asked. Requests from high-balance, high-spend customers count for more than requests from dormant accounts.
  • Who didn't answer. Businesses that left, or never activated, because cards were pre-funded can't request credit. The survey under-counts them.
  • Why they asked. "Credit card" might mean rewards, working capital, or simply not pre-funding. Each points to a different product.
  • What they use today. If customers already run payroll elsewhere happily, the switching cost is high even if they say they want it.

3.2Credit cards

What customers are asking for

The ability to spend on a Venn card without pre-funding it: pay for ads, inventory and travel now, and settle later. Most want convenience, not borrowing (Section 2: 81% of SMEs that skipped financing said they didn't need it).

Serves most: Cross-border founders Finance-led scale-ups

Value: customer growth

  • Wins businesses that need card credit and today choose Float, Loop, Ramp or a bank card instead.
  • Reduces churn risk among existing customers as rivals court them, especially after Ramp's July 2026 launch.
  • Every card purchase is a revenue-generating transaction, so higher spend keeps accounts "active".

Value: revenue (ARPU)

  • Removes the ceiling on interchange, the stream that grows with a customer's business (Section 1.2).
  • Larger limits push customers toward Pro, where cashback is uncapped and FX is cheapest.
  • A charge card paid from the Venn account keeps balances on Venn to settle the bill.

Money flow it brings · example 20-person firm

+$240K

card spend a year moved onto Venn, if true spend is $40K a month but the customer keeps only $20K in Venn.

Worth roughly +$3,000 a year to Venn on the Plus plan, using the interchange assumptions in Section 1.2.

Link to long-term strategy

A default finance platform can't cap spend at deposits; every rival that wants that title offers credit. A charge card paid in full each month keeps it "10x cheaper": no revolving interest (business cards average 19%), no personal guarantee.

What it would take · risks

  • A credit or charge program with the issuing partner (H4: the disclosure already says "charge card").
  • Capital to fund receivables. Float raised close to CA$100M in debt facilities for this.
  • Underwriting on Venn's cash-flow data without slowing 5-minute onboarding.
  • Credit losses, and higher regulatory scrutiny.

Verdict · Credit cards

The highest-value request, and the one Venn can least afford to skip.

ButIt is also the riskiest. The form matters: a charge card underwritten on Venn balances limits losses and capital needs.

3.3Payroll

What customers are asking for

Run payroll where the money already is: pay employees, remit CPP, EI and income tax to CRA, and issue T4s and ROEs, without a separate tool and a transfer between accounts.

Serves most: Operating SMBs with field teams Finance-led scale-ups

Value: customer growth

  • Payroll runs every two weeks, so it guarantees recurring transactions and active accounts.
  • Could attract domestic SMBs that don't need cheap FX, widening Venn's appeal.
  • Switching payroll mid-year is painful, so new adoption will be slow.

Value: revenue (ARPU)

  • A fee line: Wagepoint charges $40 a month plus $6 per employee, about $1,900 a year for 20 staff.
  • Funds land in Venn before each pay run, lifting balances and making Venn the primary account.
  • Payroll itself earns little interchange or FX.

$1.4M

of payroll a year leaving from Venn, at Canada's average weekly earnings of $1,344.

Large flow, thin direct margin. The value is primacy and stickiness more than revenue.

Link to long-term strategy

The strongest fit with "default banking platform": the business whose payroll leaves from Venn treats Venn as its main bank. It also answers H2 directly, if many customers are FX-first rather than primary-account users.

What it would take · risks

  • A crowded field: 16+ Canadian providers, many already integrated with QuickBooks and Xero.

Why anchor to the balance first

5.3MVP scope

The MVP proves one thing: existing customers will move more spend onto Venn when the balance ceiling is removed, and repay reliably. Everything else waits.

In the MVP

  • Eligibility rules on balance history, inflows and account age
  • In-app offer and acceptance with plain-language terms
  • Authorization against available credit, keeping all card controls
  • Monthly statement and auto-pay from the Venn account
  • Failed-payment handling: retry, pause cards, collections queue
  • Risk console: exposure view, limit changes, freeze
  • Accounting sync for Charge transactions and statements

Not in the MVP

  • Cash-flow underwriting model (Phase 2)
  • USD Charge and multi-currency statements
  • Applications from new customers
  • Custom payment terms (15-day, 60-day)
  • Revolving balances or interest
  • Charge-specific rewards

Dependencies

  • Issuing partner: credit program approval and terms
  • Funding: balance-sheet capital for beta receivables
  • Risk: eligibility rules, loss budget, limit policy
  • Legal and compliance: cardholder terms and disclosures
  • Support: playbooks for statements and failed payments

5.6Risks and experiments

A pre-mortem: it's six months after launch and Charge has failed. These are the most likely reasons, how we'd spot them early, and what we'd do.

Why it failedEarly signalResponse
Customers didn't need it (H1 false)Few low-funds declines in the data; low acceptanceCaught at Gate A, before building
The offer wasn't compellingAcceptance under 40%Test limit size, copy and placement
Customers accepted but didn't spend moreLow utilization; no lift vs holdoutInterview non-users; target customers with declines first

Experiment 1 · Incrementality

Randomly hold back 20% of pre-qualified customers from the offer. Compare spend, retention and deposits over 90 days. This is the only way to know Charge caused the lift.

Experiment 2 · Limit sizing

Test two limit multiples (1.5x and 2x balance) on similar customers. Measure the spend gained per dollar of exposure, which tells risk where to set Phase 2 limits.
